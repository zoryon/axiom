# Intermediate Value Theorem

The Intermediate Value Theorem converts continuity into an existence guarantee. It does not tell you where a root is or whether it is unique; it tells you that a continuous graph connecting two output levels cannot teleport past values in between.

## The theorem

If `f` is continuous on `[a,b]` and `N` lies between `f(a)` and `f(b)`, then there exists `c∈[a,b]` such that `f(c)=N`. If `N` lies strictly between the endpoint values, one may take `c∈(a,b)`.

## Root-existence form

If `f` is continuous on `[a,b]` and `f(a)` and `f(b)` have opposite signs, then some `c∈(a,b)` satisfies `f(c)=0`. This is the common root-existence corollary. The sign change is sufficient, not necessary: a function may touch zero without changing sign.

## Existence versus location

IVT proves at least one solution exists. Bisection turns the theorem into an algorithm: repeatedly halve an interval whose endpoints have opposite signs. The intervals shrink while retaining a root. Numerical computing later studies convergence, error bounds and floating-point behavior of this procedure.

## Existence versus uniqueness

A sign change may hide several roots. To prove uniqueness you need additional information, such as strict monotonicity. Keep the logical claims separate: IVT gives existence; monotonicity can add uniqueness.

## Worked reasoning

For `f(x)=x³+x-1`, `f(0)=-1` and `f(1)=1`; polynomials are continuous, so IVT guarantees a root in `(0,1)`. If later we note the derivative is `3x²+1>0`, strict increase proves that root is unique—but derivative reasoning is a separate theorem.

## Hypothesis discipline

Continuity on the whole interval matters. A discontinuous function can jump from negative to positive without ever equaling zero. Endpoint values alone are not enough.
